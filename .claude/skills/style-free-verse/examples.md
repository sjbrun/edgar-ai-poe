# Free Verse Examples

## Example 1 (Walt Whitman, "When I Heard the Learn'd Astronomer," 1865, public domain)
```
When I heard the learn'd astronomer,
When the proofs, the figures, were ranged in columns before me,
When I was shown the charts and diagrams, to add, divide, and measure them,
When I sitting heard the astronomer where he lectured with much applause in the lecture-room,
How soon unaccountable I became tired and sick,
Till rising and gliding out I wander'd off by myself,
In the mystical moist night-air, and from time to time,
Look'd up in perfect silence at the stars.
```
**Why it works:** Repeating "When..." (anaphora) gives the poem structure without rhyme. The first four lines grow longer and more cluttered, like the lecture. The second half moves outdoors, and the last line is short, quiet, and almost perfectly iambic, so its form enacts the "perfect silence."

## Example 2 (Carl Sandburg, "Fog," 1916, public domain)
```
The fog comes
on little cat feet.

It sits looking
over harbor and city
on silent haunches
and then moves on.
```
**Why it works:** A single extended metaphor (fog as cat) in very short lines. The line breaks slow the reader down to the fog's pace, and the plain final line "and then moves on" lets the poem leave as quietly as the fog does.

## Example 3 (Walt Whitman, "A Noiseless Patient Spider," 1868, public domain)
```
A noiseless patient spider,
I mark'd where on a little promontory it stood isolated,
Mark'd how to explore the vacant vast surrounding,
It launch'd forth filament, filament, filament, out of itself,
Ever unreeling them, ever tirelessly speeding them.

And you O my soul where you stand,
Surrounded, detached, in measureless oceans of space,
Ceaselessly musing, venturing, throwing, seeking the spheres to connect them,
Till the bridge you will need be form'd, till the ductile anchor hold,
Till the gossamer thread you fling catch somewhere, O my soul.
```
**Why it works:** Two stanzas mirror each other: first the spider, then the soul. Free verse lets the second stanza's lines stretch out like thread being cast. Repetition and lists ("filament, filament, filament"; "musing, venturing, throwing, seeking") give the rhythm, not meter. The final "Till… Till…" builds suspense without resolving it, so the poem ends still reaching.

## Example 4 (William Carlos Williams, "The Red Wheelbarrow," 1923, public domain)
```
so much depends
upon

a red wheel
barrow

glazed with rain
water

beside the white
chickens
```
**Why it works:** One sentence broken into four two-line stanzas (three words, then one). The line breaks do all the work. They split compound words ("wheel / barrow," "rain / water") so the reader sees each part. It's a key Imagist poem: a plain object shown precisely, with no comment except the opening claim that "so much depends" on it.

## Example 5 (Stephen Crane, "In the Desert," from *The Black Riders*, 1895, public domain)
```
In the desert
I saw a creature, naked, bestial,
Who, squatting upon the ground,
Held his heart in his hands,
And ate of it.
I said, "Is it good, friend?"
"It is bitter—bitter," he answered;
"But I like it
Because it is bitter,
And because it is my heart."
```
**Why it works:** Early American free verse as a short parable. The lines are plain and clipped, with no imagery beyond one shocking scene. A short exchange of dialogue delivers the meaning. The line breaks in the final answer ("But I like it / Because it is bitter, / And because it is my heart") slow the reply to give each reason its own weight.
