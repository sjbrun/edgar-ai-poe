# Style: Blank Verse

## Hard rules
- **Unrhymed:** no end-rhyme scheme. (An occasional rhyming couplet to close a scene or speech is a Shakespearean exception.)
- **Iambic pentameter:** five iambic feet (da-DUM ×5), about 10 syllables per line
- Don't confuse it with **free verse**: blank verse has strict meter, free verse has none

## Conventions
- **Variations keep it alive:** an initial trochee ("TO-morrow"), feminine endings (an extra unstressed 11th syllable), and occasional spondees for weight. A perfectly regular line after line sounds mechanical
- **Enjambment:** sentences run across line breaks, so the verse reads like heightened speech instead of a string of end-stopped lines
- **Caesura:** pauses inside the line (marked by punctuation) vary the rhythm
- Organized in **verse paragraphs** instead of stanzas
- The natural meter for English drama (Marlowe, Shakespeare), epic (Milton), meditative and conversational poems (Wordsworth, Coleridge), and dramatic monologue (Browning, Tennyson, Frost)

## Themes & imagery
Wide-ranging: epic and heroic subjects, drama and soliloquy, philosophical reflection, rural narratives, and conversation. It suits extended thought and storytelling better than short lyric bursts.

## Tone & vocabulary
Should sound like a person thinking or speaking aloud, raised but natural. Modern blank verse uses everyday diction (Frost). Scan every line before presenting it. A good test is to read it aloud and check that the stresses fall on the even syllables without distorting how the words are normally pronounced.
