# Sea Shanty Examples

All five songs are traditional (public domain). They come from the owner's collection in Google Drive (Lyrics › Sea Shanties). Where the owner's doc has both their own arrangement and a "trad" version, the arrangement is shown here.

Notation: lines in *(parentheses)* are crew echoes. `CREW:` marks refrain lines sung by everyone.

## Example 1: "Haul on the Bowline" (traditional): short-drag shanty
```
Haul on the bowline, me bully ship's a-rolling
CREW: Haul on the bowline, the bowline HAUL!

Haul on the bowline, Kitty is me darlin'
CREW: Haul on the bowline, the bowline HAUL!

Haul on the bowline, Kitty lives in Liverpool
CREW: Haul on the bowline, the bowline HAUL!

Haul on the bowline, so early in the mornin'
CREW: Haul on the bowline, the bowline HAUL!

Haul on the bowline, the old man is a-growlin'
CREW: Haul on the bowline, the bowline HAUL!

Haul on the bowline, the mate he is a-roarin'
CREW: Haul on the bowline, the bowline HAUL!

Haul on the bowline, it's a far cry to payday
CREW: Haul on the bowline, the bowline HAUL!

Haul on the bowline, oh haul and make her go
CREW: Haul on the bowline, the bowline HAUL!
```
**Why it works:** This is the simplest shanty form. Every solo line starts with the same phrase, so the shantyman only has to make up the second half, and the song can go on as long as the job does. The crew pulls once, on the shouted "HAUL!" There's no plot, just quick snapshots of shipboard life (the girl, the captain, the mate, payday). The words serve the rhythm.

## Example 2: "The Black Ball Line" (traditional, owner's arrangement): halyard shanty
```
I'll sing you a song of the Black Ball Line
CREW: To me way, hey, hey, oh rah oh!
The finest ships that sailed the brine
CREW: Hurrah for the Black Ball Line!

I served me time in the Black Ball Line
CREW: To me way, hey, hey, oh rah oh!
In the Black Ball Line I served me time
CREW: Hurrah for the Black Ball Line!

The Black Ball ships they are good and true
CREW: To me way, hey, hey, oh rah oh!
They are the ships for me and you
CREW: Hurrah for the Black Ball Line!

Once there was a Black Ball ship
CREW: To me way, hey, hey, oh rah oh!
At fourteen knots an hour could clip
CREW: Hurrah for the Black Ball Line!

You will surely find a rich gold mine
CREW: To me way, hey, hey, oh rah oh!
Just take a trip in the Black Ball Line
CREW: Hurrah for the Black Ball Line!

Oh take a trip to Liverpool
CREW: To me way, hey, hey, oh rah oh!
To Liverpool, that Yankee school
CREW: Hurrah for the Black Ball Line!

The Yankee sailors you'll see there
CREW: To me way, hey, hey, oh rah oh!
With red-topped boots and short-cut hair
CREW: Hurrah for the Black Ball Line!

I've sung my song of the Black Ball Line
CREW: To me way, hey, hey, oh rah oh!
The finest ships that sailed the brine
CREW: Hurrah for the Black Ball Line!
```
**Why it works:** This is the classic halyard pattern of solo, refrain, solo, refrain, which gives two hauls per verse. The solo lines form rhyming couplets (Line / brine, true / you, ship / clip), and the two refrains are fixed. The first refrain is pure vocables ("way, hey, hey"), and the second names the subject. The arrangement is circular: "I'll sing you a song" → "I've sung my song." It's a brag song about a shipping line. (The owner's "trad" version uses the shorter refrain "To me way, hey, hey, yah!" and adds a verse about tinkers, tailors, and shoemakers "all shipped for sailors on the Black Ball crew.")

## Example 3: "Paddy Lay Back" (traditional, owner's arrangement): capstan shanty with echoes
```
'Twas a cold and dreary morning in December (December)
And all of me money it was spent (it was spent)
And where the hell it went I can't remember (remember)
So down to the shippin' office I went (off I went)

[Chorus]
Paddy lay back (Paddy lay back)! Take in the slack (take in the slack)!
Take a turn around the capstan, heave a pawwwwwwwl!
About ship, stations, boys, be handy (be handy)!
We're bound for Valparaiso 'round the Horn!

That day there was a great demand for sailors (for sailors)
For the Colonies, for 'Frisco and for France (for France)
So I joined a limey barque they called the Hotspur (the Hotspur)
And got paralytic drunk on me advance (vance vance)

'Twas on the quarterdeck where first I saw them (saw them)
Such an ugly bunch I'd never seen before (afore)
For there was a bum and stiff from every quarter (quarter)
And it made me poor old heart feel sick and sore (sore sore)

Well I asked the mate which watch was mine-oh (mine-oh)
He said he'd soon see which watch was which (watch which)
Oh he blew me down and he kicked me in the stern-oh (stern-oh)
Callin' me a dirty lousy son of a bitch (son of a bitch!)

Oh it was then I'd made me mind up I would leave her (leave her)
I'd get a job and live me life ashore (on the shore)
So I jumped overboard and swam ashore boys (shore boys)
And in the English bar I found a whore (whore whore)

But Jimmy the crimp he knew a thing or two sir (two sir)
And quickly I was outward bound again (once again)
On a limey to the chinches for guano (guano)
And here I am a-singin' the old refrain (sing it again!)
```
**Why it works:** The crew echoes the end of each solo line, which keeps everyone singing and allows jokes: the broken "(vance vance)," the shouted "(son of a bitch!)," and the self-aware "(sing it again!)." The chorus is full of real capstan orders ("take in the slack," "heave a pawl"), and the drawn-out "pawwwwl" gives the crew a long push. The verses follow the classic sailor's cycle: broke ashore, drunk on the advance, bullied by the mate, escapes, gets crimped, and ends up back at sea.

## Example 4: "Off to Sea Once More" (traditional): forebitter
```
When first I landed in Liverpool, I went upon the spree
With money at last I spent it fast, I got drunk as drunk could be
And when my money was all gone, 'twas then I wanted more
But a man must be blind to make up his mind to go to sea once more

[Chorus]
Once more, boys, once more, go to sea once more
A man must be blind to make up his mind to go to sea once more

He shipped me on board of a whaling barque, it was bound for the Greenland seas
Where the cold winds blow through frost and the snow, oh Jamaica rum would freeze
And worse to bear, I'd no hard-weather gear, for I'd spent all my money ashore
'Twas then I wished that I was dead, and could go to sea no more

…

So come all you bold seafarin' men who listen to my song
Now it's when you come off of them long trips, I'd have you not go wrong
Take my advice, drink no strong drink, don't go sleeping with no whores
Get married instead, spend all night in bed, and go to sea no more
```
**Why it works:** A forebitter isn't for work, so its lines are long and story-driven, with internal rhymes ("money at last I spent it fast," "blind / mind," "bear / gear"). The story shows sailors' grievances (spending all your pay, being robbed, the boarding-house master, the brutal whaling voyage). It ends with a classic "come all you…" warning, whose punchline is to get married and stay home. The chorus line "go to sea once more" becomes "go to sea no more" at the end.

## Example 5: "All for me Grog" (traditional): forebitter / drinking song
```
[Chorus]
All for me grog, me jolly jolly grog
All for me beer and tobacco
For I spent all me tin on the lasses drinkin' gin
And across the western ocean I must wander

Where is me boots, me noggin', noggin' boots?
All gone for beer and tobacco
For the heels they are worn out and the toes are kicked about
And the soles are looking out for better weather

Where is me shirt, me noggin', noggin' shirt?
All gone for beer and tobacco
For the collar is wore out, and the front is knocked about
And the tail is looking out for better weather

…

I'm sick in the head I haven't been to bed
Since first I came ashore with me plunder
I see centipedes and snakes and I'm full of pains and aches
And I think I'll take a trip out over yonder
```
**Why it works:** The song uses a list frame: each verse asks after another possession ("Where is me ___, me noggin', noggin' ___?") and gets the same answer, "All gone for beer and tobacco." Each verse inventories three parts of the item (heels / toes / soles), with the last one "looking out for better weather," which is both a pun and a refrain. The internal rhymes (out / about, tin / gin) make it easy to sing. The owner's version has a bawdy verse with clapped-out words for the crowd to fill in, a traditional pub-singing game. Like most shanties, the song ends with the broke sailor heading back to sea.
