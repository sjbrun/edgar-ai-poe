# Ballad Examples

## Example 1 ("Sir Patrick Spens," traditional Scottish ballad, opening stanza, public domain; versions vary)
```
The king sits in Dunfermline town,
Drinking the blude-red wine:
"O whar will I get guid sailor,
To sail this ship of mine?"
```
**Why it works:** It opens in the middle of the scene with no backstory. The formulaic "blude-red wine" also foreshadows violence. Dialogue begins by line 3, and the ABCB rhyme (wine / mine) uses the 4-3-4-3 ballad meter. The rest of the ballad skips straight to the doomed voyage and never explains why.

## Example 2 (Samuel Taylor Coleridge, *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner*, 1798/1817, public domain)
```
It is an ancient Mariner,
And he stoppeth one of three.
"By thy long grey beard and glittering eye,
Now wherefore stopp'st thou me?

…

Water, water, every where,
And all the boards did shrink;
Water, water, every where,
Nor any drop to drink.
```
**Why it works:** A literary ballad that deliberately imitates the folk form, with an archaic "stoppeth," an abrupt start, and dialogue. The second stanza shows how ballad repetition works: the repeated line imitates the endless sea and makes the stanza easy to memorize.

## Example 3 ("Lord Randal," traditional Scottish/English ballad, first two stanzas, public domain; versions vary)
```
"O where hae ye been, Lord Randal, my son?
O where hae ye been, my handsome young man?"
"I hae been to the wild wood; mother, make my bed soon,
For I'm weary wi' hunting, and fain wald lie down."

"Where gat ye your dinner, Lord Randal, my son?
Where gat ye your dinner, my handsome young man?"
"I din'd wi' my true-love; mother, make my bed soon,
For I'm weary wi' hunting, and fain wald lie down."
```
**Why it works:** The whole ballad is dialogue, with no narrator. This is the classic example of **incremental repetition**: each stanza keeps the frame and changes one detail (where he went, what he ate, what happened to his hounds). The listener figures out that he's been poisoned before anyone says it. The unchanging refrain "make my bed soon" turns from tiredness into a deathbed request.

## Example 4 ("The Twa Corbies," traditional Scottish ballad, first two stanzas, public domain; versions vary)
```
As I was walking all alane,
I heard twa corbies making a mane;
The tane unto the t'other say,
"Where sall we gang and dine to-day?"

"In behint yon auld fail dyke,
I wot there lies a new slain knight;
And naebody kens that he lies there,
But his hawk, his hound, and lady fair."
```
**Why it works:** It uses rhyming couplets (AABB) instead of the usual ABCB, which shows the ballad stanza is flexible. A narrator frames the scene in one line, then lets two crows (corbies) tell the story in cold, practical dialogue. A murder gets hinted at, not explained: the lady knows where the body is and has moved on. The bleak, unsentimental tone is typical of the Border ballads.

## Example 5 (John Keats, "La Belle Dame sans Merci," first and last stanzas, 1819, public domain)
```
O what can ail thee, knight-at-arms,
Alone and palely loitering?
The sedge has withered from the lake,
And no birds sing.

…

And this is why I sojourn here,
Alone and palely loitering,
Though the sedge is withered from the lake,
And no birds sing.
```
**Why it works:** A literary ballad that changes the stanza: the fourth line is cut to two beats ("And no birds sing"), so every stanza ends in a dying fall. It uses the traditional ballad's question-and-answer opening, a supernatural lover, and a **circular structure**: the knight's last words echo the stranger's first question. The story ends where it started, and the knight is still trapped.
