# Villanelle Examples

## Example 1 (Edwin Arlington Robinson, "The House on the Hill," 1894, public domain)
```
They are all gone away,              A1
   The House is shut and still,      b
There is nothing more to say.        A2

Through broken walls and gray
   The winds blow bleak and shrill:
They are all gone away.              A1

Nor is there one to-day
   To speak them good or ill:
There is nothing more to say.        A2

Why is it then we stray
   Around the sunken sill?
They are all gone away,              A1

And our poor fancy-play
   For them is wasted skill:
There is nothing more to say.        A2

There is ruin and decay
   In the House on the Hill:
They are all gone away,              A1
There is nothing more to say.        A2
```
**Why it works:** It follows the form exactly, but in short trimeter lines, which shows that pentameter isn't required. The two refrains are flat statements of absence, and their repetition becomes the poem's meaning: the speaker keeps returning to the house the way the lines return. "There is nothing more to say" is ironic, said again and again in a poem that keeps talking.

## Example 2 (original)
```
I leave the porch light burning every night
though every road is empty in the rain,
as if the dark could bring you back to sight.

The moths come knocking, frantic for the light;
they beat their dusty wings against the pane.
I leave the porch light burning every night.

The neighbors say it's time, and they are right,
that waiting is a habit, and in vain,
as if the dark could bring you back to sight.

I tell them that I like the evening bright,
the way it shows the puddles in the lane.
I leave the porch light burning every night.

I do not say I listen, holding tight,
for footsteps I would know, and know again,
as if the dark could bring you back to sight.

So let them talk. The bulb is burning white.
It costs so little, and it eases pain.
I leave the porch light burning every night
as if the dark could bring you back to sight.
```
**Why it works:** The refrains change meaning as they return. "As if the dark could…" first sounds like hope, then like the neighbors' criticism, then like the speaker's private admission. The moths at the window echo the speaker, also drawn to a light that can't give them what they want.

## Example 3 (Oscar Wilde, "Theocritus: A Villanelle," 1881, public domain)
```
O singer of Persephone!                A1
   In the dim meadows desolate         b
Dost thou remember Sicily?             A2

Still through the ivy flits the bee
   Where Amaryllis lies in state;
O Singer of Persephone!                A1

Simaetha calls on Hecate
   And hears the wild dogs at the gate;
Dost thou remember Sicily?             A2

Still by the light and laughing sea
   Poor Polypheme bemoans his fate;
O Singer of Persephone!                A1

And still in boyish rivalry
   Young Daphnis challenges his mate;
Dost thou remember Sicily?             A2

Slim Lacon keeps a goat for thee,
   For thee the jocund shepherds wait;
O Singer of Persephone!
Dost thou remember Sicily?
```
**Why it works:** A Victorian villanelle in tetrameter, from the period when English poets revived the form. It's addressed to the dead Greek pastoral poet Theocritus, and each tercet names a character from his poems who is "still" alive in them. The refrains are an address and a question that's never answered, so the form's repetition becomes calling out to the dead. The light, songlike movement is closer to the villanelle's pastoral origins than to its modern use for obsession.

## Example 4 (W. E. Henley, "A Villanelle," 1888, public domain)
```
A dainty thing's the Villanelle.
   Sly, musical, a jewel in rhyme,
It serves its purpose passing well.

A double-clappered silver bell
   That must be made to clink in chime,
A dainty thing's the Villanelle;

And if you wish to flute a spell,
   Or ask a meeting 'neath the lime,
It serves its purpose passing well.

You must not ask of it the swell
   Of organs grandiose and sublime—
A dainty thing's the Villanelle;

And, filled with sweetness, as a shell
   Is filled with sound, it launches Time.
It serves its purpose passing well.

Still fair to see and good to smell
   As in the quaintness of its prime,
A dainty thing's the Villanelle,
It serves its purpose passing well.
```
**Why it works:** A villanelle about the villanelle, which makes it a working demonstration of the rules. The "double-clappered silver bell" describes the two alternating refrains exactly. It also shows the Victorian view of the form as light and decorative ("You must not ask of it the swell / Of organs"). Twentieth-century poets like Thomas and Bishop would overturn that view by using it for grief.
