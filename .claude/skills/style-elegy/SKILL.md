# Style: Elegy

## Hard rules
- A serious, reflective poem of mourning, usually for a specific person, but also for a lost time, place, community, or way of life
- No fixed meter or rhyme in English. (The classical "elegiac couplet" of Greek and Latin and the English "elegiac stanza", an ABAB quatrain in iambic pentameter, are optional choices.)

## Conventions
The traditional elegy moves through three stages. They don't all need equal weight, but the arc should be there:
1. **Lament:** expressing grief and loss, sometimes with anger or disbelief
2. **Praise:** remembering and honoring the dead, often through specific details of who they were
3. **Consolation:** some resolution, such as faith, the continuity of nature, memory, the poem as a memorial, or simply acceptance. Modern elegies often resist or undercut consolation

Other conventions:
- The **pastoral elegy** (Milton's "Lycidas," Shelley's "Adonais") casts the mourner and the dead as shepherds, and nature mourns with them
- The speaker often questions fate, God, or death itself
- Repetition and refrain can work like ritual keening
- Specific, ordinary details of the dead (a habit, a phrase, an object) hit harder than general praise

## Themes & imagery
Death and mortality, memory, absence, seasons and the passing of time, dusk and night, empty rooms and places, and the indifferent continuation of the world (the sea, the stars, other people's ordinary lives).

## Tone & vocabulary
Grave, tender, and restrained. Grief comes through more strongly in understatement than in exclamation. Avoid greeting-card clichés ("in a better place," "forever in our hearts") unless you deliberately subvert them. When writing for a real person the user is mourning, be gentle and use the details the user provides.
