# Elegy Examples

## Example 1 (Thomas Gray, "Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard," stanzas 1–2, 1751, public domain)
```
The curfew tolls the knell of parting day,
The lowing herd wind slowly o'er the lea,
The plowman homeward plods his weary way,
And leaves the world to darkness and to me.

Now fades the glimm'ring landscape on the sight,
And all the air a solemn stillness holds,
Save where the beetle wheels his droning flight,
And drowsy tinklings lull the distant folds;
```
**Why it works:** This is the model of the "elegiac stanza": ABAB quatrains in iambic pentameter. The slow, heavy monosyllables ("The plowman homeward plods his weary way") act out the dusk, and the end of day prepares a meditation on death. The poem mourns ordinary villagers instead of one famous person, so it's an elegy for a whole class of forgotten lives.

## Example 2 (Alfred, Lord Tennyson, "Break, Break, Break," first and last stanzas, 1842, public domain)
```
Break, break, break,
   On thy cold gray stones, O Sea!
And I would that my tongue could utter
   The thoughts that arise in me.

…

Break, break, break,
   At the foot of thy crags, O Sea!
But the tender grace of a day that is dead
   Will never come back to me.
```
**Why it works:** Written after the death of Tennyson's friend Arthur Hallam. The three heavy stresses of "Break, break, break" sound like waves and like grief. The sea goes on, indifferent. The speaker can't put his loss into words, and the final stanza refuses consolation: the lost day "will never come back." It shows a modern-leaning elegy that stops at lament.

## Example 3 (John Milton, "Lycidas," opening, 1638, public domain)
```
Yet once more, O ye laurels, and once more
Ye myrtles brown, with ivy never sere,
I come to pluck your berries harsh and crude,
And with forc'd fingers rude
Shatter your leaves before the mellowing year.
Bitter constraint and sad occasion dear
Compels me to disturb your season due;
For Lycidas is dead, dead ere his prime,
Young Lycidas, and hath not left his peer.
```
**Why it works:** This is the model **pastoral elegy**, mourning Milton's drowned college friend Edward King. The poet picks unripe berries, which stands for writing before he feels ready, and that matches a life cut off "ere his prime." The repetition ("dead, dead… Young Lycidas") is grief breaking through formal verse. Over the full poem it follows the classic elegy arc: lament, questioning why, and finally consolation ("Weep no more, woeful shepherds").

## Example 4 (Ben Jonson, "On My First Son," 1616, public domain)
```
Farewell, thou child of my right hand, and joy;
My sin was too much hope of thee, lov'd boy.
Seven years thou wert lent to me, and I thee pay,
Exacted by thy fate, on the just day.
O, could I lose all father now! For why
Will man lament the state he should envy?
To have so soon 'scap'd world's and flesh's rage,
And if no other misery, yet age?
Rest in soft peace, and, ask'd, say, "Here doth lie
Ben Jonson his best piece of poetry."
For whose sake henceforth all his vows be such,
As what he loves may never like too much.
```
**Why it works:** A short, personal elegy in heroic couplets for Jonson's seven-year-old son, also named Benjamin, which in Hebrew means "child of the right hand." The son is a loan repaid "on the just day," a debt image that is formal and painful at once. The poet tries the standard consolation (the child escaped age and misery) and admits it doesn't quite work ("O, could I lose all father now!"). The son as "his best piece of poetry" shows the elegy's familiar move of making the poem a monument.

## Example 5 (Walt Whitman, "O Captain! My Captain!," stanza 1, 1865, public domain)
```
O Captain! my Captain! our fearful trip is done,
The ship has weather'd every rack, the prize we sought is won,
The port is near, the bells I hear, the people all exulting,
While follow eyes the steady keel, the vessel grim and daring;
     But O heart! heart! heart!
       O the bleeding drops of red,
         Where on the deck my Captain lies,
           Fallen cold and dead.
```
**Why it works:** A public elegy for Abraham Lincoln, built as one **extended metaphor**: the ship is the nation, the voyage is the Civil War, the captain is Lincoln. The long, celebrating lines suddenly give way to short, indented ones, so grief cuts through the victory. Unusually for Whitman, it rhymes and repeats a refrain ("Fallen cold and dead"), which made it easy to memorize and recite in public.
