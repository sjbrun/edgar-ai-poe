## Example 1 (Matsuo Bashō, public domain; translation varies)
```
An old silent pond
A frog jumps into the pond—
Splash! Silence again.
```
**Why it works:** The still pond and the sudden splash are set side by side. The season word "frog" signals spring.

## Example 2 (Matsuo Bashō, 1689, public domain; literal translation)
```
natsukusa ya
tsuwamonodomo ga
yume no ato

summer grasses—
all that remains
of warriors' dreams
```
**Why it works:** Written at Hiraizumi, the site of an old battle. The cutting word *ya* after "summer grasses" sets up a pause, and the second part reveals what the grass covers. The season word (summer grasses) does double duty: growth and life now, over death and ambition then. Note that the translation doesn't keep 5-7-5. Good English haiku translations favor the image over syllable count.

## Example 3 (Yosa Buson, 18th century, public domain; literal translation)
```
haru no umi
hinemosu notari
notari kana

the spring sea—
all day long rising and falling,
rising and falling
```
**Why it works:** The repeated *notari notari* (a slow, swelling motion) imitates the waves, so sound carries the meaning. Nothing happens, and that's the point: the haiku captures a whole lazy spring day in one rhythm. Buson was a painter as well as a poet, and his haiku tend toward this wide, pictorial view.

## Example 4 (Kobayashi Issa, 1819, public domain; literal translation)
```
tsuyu no yo wa
tsuyu no yo nagara
sa nagara

this world of dew
is a world of dew,
and yet… and yet…
```
**Why it works:** Written after the death of Issa's young daughter. "World of dew" is a Buddhist image for the fleeting nature of life, and the second line accepts it. The third line refuses to let go anyway. The haiku is almost entirely repetition. The emotion lives in the unfinished "and yet," which shows how a haiku can hold grief through understatement.

## Example 5 (Masaoka Shiki, 1895, public domain; literal translation)
```
kaki kueba
kane ga naru nari
Hōryūji

I bite a persimmon
and a bell rings out—
Hōryūji
```
**Why it works:** Two sensations happen at once (taste and sound), and the place name lands at the end like the bell. Shiki, who reformed the form and gave it the name "haiku," pushed for *shasei* (sketching from life): a real, ordinary moment instead of literary allusion. The persimmon is an autumn season word.
