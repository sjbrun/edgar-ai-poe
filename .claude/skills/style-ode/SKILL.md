# Style: Ode

## Hard rules
- Addresses or celebrates a single subject (a person, object, idea, season, or place), often speaking to it directly ("O…," "Thou…")
- Lyric and elevated: an ode praises and contemplates its subject. It doesn't just describe it
- Written in stanzas. The stanza pattern depends on the variant below

### Variants
| Variant | Structure |
|---|---|
| Pindaric | Triads of *strophe* and *antistrophe* (identical in form) followed by an *epode* (a different form). Grand and public |
| Horatian | Repeated identical stanzas with a regular meter and rhyme. Calm, reflective, private |
| Irregular (English) | Stanzas vary in length, meter, and rhyme as the thought requires (e.g. Wordsworth's "Intimations" ode) |

The great Romantic odes (Keats) use a fixed stanza of their own design, usually 10–11 lines of iambic pentameter that mix a quatrain with a sestet-like rhyme. If the user doesn't name a variant, default to a Keatsian or Horatian stanza with consistent meter and rhyme, and say so in the notes.

## Conventions
- Apostrophe: speaking directly to the subject, which can be absent, abstract, or inanimate
- Movement of thought: the poem often begins in praise or description, deepens into meditation, and ends in insight, acceptance, or paradox
- Rich sensory detail, heaped up across the stanza
- Odes often find something larger in the subject, such as mortality, art, or permanence and change

## Themes & imagery
Seasons, nature (birds, wind, sky), art objects, abstract qualities (melancholy, duty, joy), heroes and occasions, and everyday objects (in modern odes, e.g. an ode to socks or tomatoes, which can be sincere or gently comic).

## Tone & vocabulary
Exalted, sincere, and lush. Ceremonial in the Pindaric ode, intimate in the Horatian. Use sensory, textured words and avoid irony unless the ode is mock-heroic. Exclamations and rhetorical questions are at home here.
