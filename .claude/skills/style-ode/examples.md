# Ode Examples

## Example 1 (John Keats, "To Autumn," stanza 1, 1820, public domain)
```
Season of mists and mellow fruitfulness,               a
   Close bosom-friend of the maturing sun;             b
Conspiring with him how to load and bless              a
   With fruit the vines that round the thatch-eves run; b
To bend with apples the moss'd cottage-trees,          c
   And fill all fruit with ripeness to the core;       d
      To swell the gourd, and plump the hazel shells   e
   With a sweet kernel; to set budding more,           d
And still more, later flowers for the bees,            c
Until they think warm days will never cease,           c
   For summer has o'er-brimm'd their clammy cells.     e
```
**Why it works:** The poem speaks directly to Autumn as a person. The whole stanza is one long, overflowing sentence of infinitives ("to load… to bend… to swell… to set"), so the syntax is as full as the season. Keats's own 11-line stanza (ababcdedcce) opens with a quatrain and then delays its resolution, which is typical of the Romantic ode. Later stanzas shift from ripeness to harvest to the end of the year, quietly moving toward mortality.

## Example 2 (John Keats, "Ode on a Grecian Urn," closing lines, 1819, public domain)
```
"Beauty is truth, truth beauty,"—that is all
Ye know on earth, and all ye need to know.
```
**Why it works:** The ode ends in paradox instead of description, which is a classic ode move. Meditating on an object (the urn) leads to a large and debatable claim about art and truth. Who speaks these lines, the urn or the poet, is a famous interpretive question.

## Example 3 (Percy Bysshe Shelley, "Ode to the West Wind," section I, 1820, public domain)
```
O wild West Wind, thou breath of Autumn's being,
Thou, from whose unseen presence the leaves dead
Are driven, like ghosts from an enchanter fleeing,

Yellow, and black, and pale, and hectic red,
Pestilence-stricken multitudes: O thou,
Who chariotest to their dark wintry bed

The winged seeds, where they lie cold and low,
Each like a corpse within its grave, until
Thine azure sister of the Spring shall blow

Her clarion o'er the dreaming earth, and fill
(Driving sweet buds like flocks to feed in air)
With living hues and odours plain and hill:

Wild Spirit, which art moving everywhere;
Destroyer and preserver; hear, oh hear!
```
**Why it works:** Apostrophe at full force: the poem calls on the wind as a god. Each section is a sonnet-length unit of **terza rima** (aba bcb cdc ded ee), and the interlocking rhyme drives forward like the wind. One long sentence runs for fourteen lines before the plea "hear, oh hear!" The wind as "destroyer and preserver" sets up the ode's movement from praise toward a personal prayer later in the poem.

## Example 4 (William Wordsworth, "Ode: Intimations of Immortality," opening, 1807, public domain): irregular
```
There was a time when meadow, grove, and stream,
The earth, and every common sight,
    To me did seem
  Apparelled in celestial light,
The glory and the freshness of a dream.
It is not now as it hath been of yore;—
    Turn wheresoe'er I may,
      By night or day,
The things which I have seen I now can see no more.
```
**Why it works:** The model of the **irregular English ode**. Line lengths change with the feeling: two-beat lines ("To me did seem," "By night or day") alternate with full pentameter and an alexandrine at the close. The subject is a lost state of mind (a child's sense of glory), not an object. The opening lament leads to the ode's long meditation and its hard-won consolation.

## Example 5 (Andrew Marvell, "An Horatian Ode upon Cromwell's Return from Ireland," opening, 1650, public domain): Horatian
```
The forward youth that would appear
Must now forsake his Muses dear,
    Nor in the shadows sing
    His numbers languishing.
```
**Why it works:** The **Horatian** model: the same four-line stanza (two tetrameter lines, then two trimeter) repeats unchanged throughout. It's restrained and cool, not ecstatic. Even when it praises a public figure, the tone stays measured and its judgment ambiguous. Compare the heat of Shelley's ode above.

## Example 6 (Ben Jonson, "To the Immortal Memory and Friendship of That Noble Pair, Sir Lucius Cary and Sir H. Morison," from stanza 7, "The Turn," 1640, public domain): Pindaric
```
It is not growing like a tree
In bulk, doth make man better be;
Or standing long an oak, three hundred year,
To fall a log at last, dry, bald, and sere:
    A lily of a day
    Is fairer far in May,
  Although it fall and die that night;
  It was the plant and flower of light.
In small proportions we just beauties see;
And in short measures life may perfect be.
```
**Why it works:** One of the first English **Pindaric** odes, which labels its parts "The Turn," "The Counter-Turn," and "The Stand" (strophe, antistrophe, and epode). The line lengths act out the argument: the long lines are the slow oak, and the short lines are the brief lily. The closing couplet says the poem's thesis (a short life can be perfect) in the elevated public voice of a Pindaric ode honoring the dead.
