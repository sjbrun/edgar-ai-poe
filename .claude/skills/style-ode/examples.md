# Ode Examples

## Example 1 (John Keats, "To Autumn," stanza 1, 1820, public domain)
```
Season of mists and mellow fruitfulness,               a
   Close bosom-friend of the maturing sun;             b
Conspiring with him how to load and bless              a
   With fruit the vines that round the thatch-eves run; b
To bend with apples the moss'd cottage-trees,          c
   And fill all fruit with ripeness to the core;       d
      To swell the gourd, and plump the hazel shells   e
   With a sweet kernel; to set budding more,           d
And still more, later flowers for the bees,            c
Until they think warm days will never cease,           c
   For summer has o'er-brimm'd their clammy cells.     e
```
**Why it works:** The poem speaks directly to Autumn as a person. The whole stanza is one long, overflowing sentence of infinitives ("to load… to bend… to swell… to set"), so the syntax is as full as the season. Keats's own 11-line stanza (ababcdedcce) opens with a quatrain and then delays its resolution, which is typical of the Romantic ode. Later stanzas shift from ripeness to harvest to the end of the year, quietly moving toward mortality.

## Example 2 (John Keats, "Ode on a Grecian Urn," closing lines, 1819, public domain)
```
"Beauty is truth, truth beauty,"—that is all
Ye know on earth, and all ye need to know.
```
**Why it works:** The ode ends in paradox instead of description, which is a classic ode move. Meditating on an object (the urn) leads to a large and debatable claim about art and truth. Who speaks these lines, the urn or the poet, is a famous interpretive question.
