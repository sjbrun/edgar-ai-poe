# Acrostic Examples

## Example 1 (Edgar Allan Poe, "An Acrostic," c. 1829, public domain)
```
Elizabeth it is in vain you say
"Love not"—thou sayest it in so sweet a way:
In vain those words from thee or L. E. L.
Zantippe's talents had enforced so well:
Ah! if that language from thy heart arise,
Breath it less gently forth—and veil thine eyes.
Endymion, recollect, when Luna tried
To cure his love—was cured of all beside—
His follie—pride—and passion—for he died.
```
**Why it works:** The first letters spell ELIZABETH, the name of the cousin it addresses. The rhymed couplets read as one flowing argument, so the constraint is almost invisible. The poem playfully refuses her advice not to love, and the acrostic form itself is a hidden dedication.

## Example 2 (Lewis Carroll, opening of the closing poem of *Through the Looking-Glass*, 1871, public domain)
```
A boat beneath a sunny sky,
Lingering onward dreamily
In an evening of July—
Children three that nestle near,
Eager eye and willing ear,
Pleased a simple tale to hear—
```
**Why it works:** The full poem spells ALICE PLEASANCE LIDDELL, the real Alice. The acrostic is so smooth that many readers never notice it, which fits a poem about a fading, half-dreamed memory.

## Example 3 (original)
```
Rooftops drum a steady tune,
All the gutters overflow;
In the puddles, clouds and moon.
Night is soft and lamps burn low.
```
**Why it works:** A short rhymed acrostic (ABAB) that spells its subject. Each line adds a new image of the rain, and the last line widens out to the mood of the whole night.
